The translator's preface

Late in this same generation, the resident Italian guild — traders long settled beside the quarry cliffs at the island's far end — needed a lock-house of their own: a place that decided, for their hall and their outposts elsewhere, who held which key, and that would not fail. Rather than invent a rite from scratch, the guild's engineers built on the Paxon protocol, which by then had a page or two of circulation among the island's clerks.

Their report, translated here, was written by three engineers: Candra, Griesemerus and Lapis Rubeus. Unlike the geometers' treatises of the earlier volumes, it contains no theorem. It contains, instead, a list of everything that went wrong between the one-page Greek description of the protocol and a building that stood up.

The guild's report is candid, occasionally rueful, and more useful to a working builder than anything else in these nine volumes.

— The Translator

The Greek parable fits on a page. The guild's lock-house took several thousand courses of masonry. Between the two lay broken chisels, consuls who did not know they had been deposed, cliffs of old records, a rite that could not be inspected by watching it work — and operators who restored the wrong scroll.
Chapter I

The Lock-House of the Guild

It is well known, the engineers begin, that a building of ordinary bricks can be made to stand through replication: keep several identical copies and have them agree. Apply an agreement rite over and over to a sequence of values and every copy ends with an identical ledger; if the values are operations on some record, every copy ends with identical records. The Paxon rite had been discussed by scholars and builders alike for over a decade.

The guild's Lock-House provided locks and stored small scrolls for its sister offices — the Great Scroll-Store and the Tabularium among them — which used it to coordinate and to keep a little of their own records. There was one such Lock-House for the guild's dealings on Paxos, and others like it at their outposts elsewhere; each was a cell. A cell had five copies, each in its own building, one of which was the master. Clients asked any copy; a copy that was not the master replied with the master's address. If the master fell, a new one was chosen automatically and carried on from its own copy of the records.1

The first Lock-House had been built on a foreign contractor's replicated record-keeping, which the report calls simply "3DB". It had a history of replication faults, and so far as the engineers knew its replication was not based on any proven rite. Given the Lock-House's importance, they decided to replace it with their own, built on Paxos.

1. The Lock-House itself was designed by the engineer Cuniculus, who suggested the replacement, and reviewed with Margarita the engineers' designs. The report calls the Lock-House Chubby, a name I have been unable to translate with dignity.

1.1 · Why It Was Harder Than a Page

The Four Difficulties
  • The page and the building. Paxos can be described in a page of pseudo-code; the engineers' implementation ran to several thousand lines. The blow-up was not the language or a verbose style, but the many features and optimizations — some published, some not — that a production building requires.
  • Proofs do not scale. The scholars were used to proving one-page rites correct. That does not work for thousands of lines; other ways of gaining confidence were needed.
  • The real world breaks more than the rite allows. A rite tolerates carefully chosen faults. Real buildings also suffer errors in the rite, bugs in the masonry and mistakes by operators.
  • Specifications move. A real building is rarely specified precisely, the specification changes during construction, and a building may "fail" because of a misunderstanding in the specification itself.
Chapter II

The Plan of the Building

Each copy of the Lock-House was three storeys. At the bottom, the replicated ledger, built on Paxos, with each copy keeping its own ledger and the rite run repeatedly so all ledgers hold identical entries in identical order. In the middle, a replicated record-vault: a snapshot of the records plus a replay-ledger of operations since. New operations are submitted to the ledger below, and applied to each copy's vault as they appear. At the top, the Lock-House, which keeps its state in the vault.

The engineers took care over the walls between storeys — for clarity, and because they expected their sister offices to want a fault-tolerant ledger, which they considered a powerful foundation. The ledger's interface had a call to submit a value; once the value entered the ledger, each copy received a callback with it. Notably, the ledger created none of its own workers; it could be called by any number of the building's workers at once. That decision, as Chapter VII shows, made it testable.

Chapter III

The Guild's Reading of Paxos

The engineers summarized the Greek rite for their fellow builders in practical terms. Copies may crash and recover; messages may be dropped; each copy has a strongroom that survives crashes. If a majority run long enough without failures, all running copies agree on one of the submitted values. The rite has three steps, repeated when things go wrong:

Paxos, as the Guild Read It
  1. Elect a coordinator. A copy that wishes to coordinate picks a sequence number higher than any it has seen — unique, because copy r with id ir among n picks the smallest such s with s mod n = ir — and sends a propose. If a majority reply that they have seen no higher number, it coordinates. Their replies are promises to reject older coordinators, and each carries the most recent value the copy has heard and the number of the coordinator it heard it from.
  2. Accept. The coordinator chooses the value from the most recent coordinator among the promises — or, if there is none, any submitted value — and sends an accept. Copies acknowledge or reject.
  3. Commit. Once a majority acknowledge, consensus is reached, and the coordinator broadcasts a commit.

The reason it works is subtle but short: a new coordinator hears from a majority, so if a previous coordinator reached consensus, at least one reply carries that value, and by induction it has the highest number among the replies.

To build a ledger, run one instance of the rite per entry (Multi-Paxos). Copies that fall behind use a catch-up mechanism. Each copy records every Paxos action in a strongroom ledger and replays it after a crash — which, as described, means five flushed writes to disk per instance (propose, promise, accept, acknowledgment, commit), and in a building whose copies stand close together, disk flushes dominate the delay. The well-known remedy: if the coordinator does not change, omit the propose. Any copy may still try to take over with a higher number, so safety is untouched. A Multi-Paxos building therefore tries to keep one coordinator — the master — for long periods, and then needs only one disk write per instance on each copy, in parallel: the master writes just after sending accept, the others just before acknowledging. Values submitted by different workers can also be batched into one instance.

Chapter IV

Chipped Chisels and Mad Kings

The core rite is well described. A fault-tolerant ledger built from it is not. Some complications come from the imperfect world — broken disks, finite storerooms — and some from additional requirements. Many have solutions intimately tied to the rite itself.

4.1 · Chipped Chisels: When the Strongroom Lies

Disks go bad — through media failure, or because an operator erases something critical. A copy that loses its strongroom may renege on promises it made, which violates a key assumption of Paxos. Corruption shows itself two ways. File contents may change: each file therefore stores a checksum of its contents. Or files may vanish — indistinguishable from a brand-new copy with an empty disk. To detect that, a new copy leaves a marker in the Great Scroll-Store when it first starts; if it ever starts again with an empty disk, it finds its own marker and knows its disk was lost.

Rebuilding a Corrupted Copy

The copy takes part in Paxos as a non-voting member: it catches up, but sends no promises and no acknowledgments. It stays so until it has observed one complete instance of Paxos that began after it started rebuilding. That extra instance ensures it cannot renege on any earlier promise.

The engineers note that, since the building can now tolerate occasional corruption, one might under some circumstances skip flushing writes altogether — something they considered but had not yet built.2

2. The checksum does not catch a file that rolls back to an older valid state; the engineers judged that unlikely, and hoped their distributed consistency checks (Chapter VI) would notice it.

4.2 · Mad Kings: Master Leases

With plain Paxos, even a read of the records must go through an instance of the rite, to be sure it sees the current state; the master cannot answer from its own copy, because the others might have elected a new master and changed the records without telling it. Reads are the bulk of all requests, so this is expensive. The remedy is a master lease: while the master holds the lease, it is guaranteed that no other copy can successfully submit values — so it may answer reads alone.

In the engineers' building, all copies implicitly grant a lease to the master of the previous instance and refuse Paxos messages from anyone else while it lasts. The master uses a shorter lease timeout than the other copies, to guard against drifting water-clocks, and periodically submits a dummy "heartbeat" value to renew. In practice masters held their leases for days at a time.

Leases exposed a stability problem. A master briefly cut off is replaced; the new master keeps a fixed sequence number across instances; meanwhile the cut-off old master, if it reaches even one other copy, may raise its own number — and on reconnecting, displace the new master; then disconnect again; and so on. Frequent master changes hurt the Lock-House's clients. So the master periodically boosts its sequence number by running a full round of Paxos, propose and all. In a loaded system, under one percent of instances ran the full rite.

4.3 · Epoch Numbers

Between receiving a request and the request updating the records, a master may lose its status — or lose it and regain it. The Lock-House required such requests to be aborted. The engineers introduced a global epoch number: two requests for it at the master receive the same value if and only if that copy was master continuously between them. It is stored in the records, and every operation is made conditional on it.

4.4 · Changing the Garrison

A real building must change which copies belong to it. Some Paxos papers point out that the rite itself can manage membership. With the core rite that is straightforward; with Multi-Paxos, corrupted disks and the rest, the engineers found that the literature neither spelled out the details nor proved them correct. They had to fill the gaps themselves, and describe the details — "relatively minor" but subtle — as beyond the scope of their report.

Chapter V

The Crumbling Cliffs

A ledger built by repeated agreement grows for ever. That needs unbounded storerooms and, worse, unbounded recovery time, since a recovering copy must replay it all. The obvious remedy is a snapshot of the records, after which the ledger leading up to it is no longer needed. The Paxos foundation knows nothing about the records, so the application must take the snapshots; the foundation, when told, truncates its ledger. On recovery a copy installs its latest snapshot and replays the rest. Snapshots are not synchronized; each copy chooses its own moments.

The literature mentions this briefly. In practice it made a copy's persistent state a ledger and a snapshot that had to be kept mutually consistent, one under the foundation's control and one in the application's format.

What the Snapshot Machinery Required
  • A snapshot handle. Each snapshot carries a handle supplied by the foundation — itself a snapshot of the Paxos state, holding the instance number the snapshot corresponds to and the group membership at that point. The application stores it with the snapshot and returns it on recovery.
  • Three phases, so the building need not freeze. The application requests a handle; takes its snapshot (perhaps in a separate worker while the copy keeps participating, which requires care, since the records are changing); then informs the foundation, which truncates.
  • Snapshots may fail. Until informed, the foundation assumes no snapshot exists — so the application can verify a snapshot and discard it, or even attempt several at once.
  • Catching up by snapshot. A lagging copy that cannot get old enough ledger entries is told to fetch a snapshot, then the remaining entries. But the leader may take a newer snapshot meanwhile, or fail after sending — so the lagging copy may need a newer snapshot, or another leader.
  • Finding snapshots. A general mechanism passes snapshot locations between copies, whether transferred directly or stored in the Great Scroll-Store.

5.1 · The MultiOp

The Lock-House needed only a simple store of name–value pairs, with insert, delete, lookup, iteration, and an atomic compare-and-swap. Atomicity was easy: submit everything the operation needs as a single value to Paxos. The engineers then generalized this into transactions without true transactions, a primitive they called MultiOp, applied atomically, through which every operation but iteration was implemented:

MultiOp
  1. guard — a list of tests, each checking one entry for presence, absence, or a given value.
  2. t_op — a list of inserts, deletes and lookups, executed if every test in the guard is true.
  3. f_op — a list executed otherwise.

When, late in construction, the epoch numbers of §4.3 turned out to be required, MultiOp paid for itself: with the epoch stored as an entry, every earlier call simply gained one more guard.

Chapter VI

The Craft of the Masons

A building that must stand for years is judged harshly. A crooked line in a letter-carver's inscription can be read around; a fault of similar gravity in a fault-tolerant building may make it unusable.

6.1 · Writing the Rite Plainly

Fault-tolerant rites are notoriously hard to express correctly even in pseudo-code, and worse when mixed into the rest of a building. The engineers wrote the core as two explicit state machines, in a small specification language of their own design, terse enough that a whole rite fits on one screen, with a compiler that translated it into their building language and automatically added logging of every transition and measurement of test coverage.

They tell a story in its favour. Near the end, they had to change their group membership rite fundamentally. A copy had gone through three states — waiting to join, joined, left — and a copy that left could never rejoin, so an intermittently failing copy could not disrupt the group for long. But intermittent failure proved far more common than expected, even in healthy copies. The rite had to become two states, in and out, freely alternating. The change took about one hour; the tests took three days.

6.2 · Checking Itself While Running

Besides liberal assertions, the master periodically submits a checksum request to the ledger. Each copy computes a checksum of its records; since the ledger orders operations identically everywhere, all should agree; the master then sends its own for comparison. This found three inconsistencies: one from an operator error; one never explained — replaying the copy's ledger showed it correct, so perhaps a random memory corruption; and one suspected illegal memory access by errant code elsewhere in the (considerable) code base, after which the engineers kept a second store of checksums and double-checked every access against it. All three were resolved by hand before the Lock-House's clients noticed.

Chapter VII

Deliberate Sabotage

It is unrealistic, the engineers write, to prove a building such as theirs correct. Beyond meticulous masonry, the best practical path is to test thoroughly, and the building was designed to be testable from the start. They describe two tests that drive the system through long sequences of random failures, each of which runs in two modes:

The Two Modes
  1. Safety mode. The test checks that the building stays consistent, but requires no progress — operations may fail or report unavailability.
  2. Liveness mode. The test checks that the building stays consistent and makes progress.

A test begins in safety mode, injecting random failures; then stops injecting, lets the building recover, and switches to liveness mode, to check that no sequence of failures has left it deadlocked.

The first test simulates a random number of copies and drives the ledger through a random sequence of network outages, delays, timeouts, crashes and recoveries, file corruptions and schedule interleavings. The failures are chosen by a random number generator from a seed given at the start, and the test runs in a single worker to remove all other nondeterminism — possible precisely because the ledger creates no workers of its own. Two runs with the same seed are identical; a failing seed is simply rerun with detailed logging in a debugger. To measure the test's strength, the engineers deliberately left known protocol bugs found in design reviews and confirmed the test caught them. When it grew stable they ran it on a farm of several hundred machines, and found more bugs — some taking weeks of simulated time, at extremely high failure rates, to appear.

The second test adds hooks to crash a copy, cut it off for a while, or make it pretend it is no longer master, and checks that the higher storeys cope. It found five subtle bugs in master failover in its first two weeks. A filesystem with failure hooks extended the method further.

7.1 · A Building That Hides Its Own Cracks

One challenge the engineers had no systematic answer for: fault-tolerant buildings are designed to mask problems, and so they mask bugs and misconfigurations too, quietly lowering their own tolerance. They once started a five-copy system with one copy's name misspelled in the initial group. The four correct copies made progress. The fifth, outside the group, ran continually in catch-up mode and also appeared healthy. The building tolerated one fault instead of two. They added procedures to detect that particular mistake, and had no way to know what else fault tolerance might be hiding.

They also record the fate of their hopes for repeatability. The ledger stayed single-worker at its core, but the Lock-House was multi-worker at its core, the record-vault had to become multi-worker to take snapshots and checksums while serving requests, and eventually even the local ledger code had to. They believe they set the right goal; the needs of the product would not let them keep it.

Chapter VIII

What Actually Went Wrong

At the time of the report, the building had logged well over 100 machine-years in production. The engineers list the unexpected failures:

What happenedConsequence and remedy
The first release had ten times as many worker threads as the old Lock-House. Under load they starved other key threads, causing timeouts, rapid master failover, mass migration of clients to the new master, which was overwhelmed — and more failovers.With the cause unknown, the team rolled one cell back to the old 3DB-based Lock-House. The rollback was undocumented and unintuitive, the operator had never used it, and no developer was present. An old snapshot was used: 15 hours of data lost; several key datasets had to be rebuilt.
Months later, the upgrade script for that cell failed because files from the failed upgrade had not been deleted.The cell ran for a few minutes on a months-old snapshot: about 30 minutes of data lost. All clients recovered.
The record-vault's semantics differed from what the Lock-House expected: an operation could succeed even if the copy lost and regained mastership during it.Substantial rework of the integration layer; the epoch numbers of §4.3, made easy by MultiOp.
Three copies found inconsistent with their cells.Found by the periodic checksums of §6.2.
The migration script from 3DB failed several times — once because a basic program was not installed on one cell.Script updated after each failure.
In their Linux 2.4 kernel, flushing a small file could hang for seconds if many writes to other files were buffered — as right after writing a snapshot.Large files are written in small chunks, each flushed, protecting the critical ledger writes.

A small number of failures in 100 machine-years would be excellent for most production buildings. The engineers judged it too high for the Lock-House. Three failures occurred during upgrade or rollback; two came from bugs since fixed; two were operator errors during rollout that lost data. The operators were very competent, but were not the builders and did not know the building's intricacies. The engineers moved to carefully written, well-tested rollout scripts that minimized operator involvement — and their most recent major release rolled out across hundreds of machines, serving live traffic, without incident.

8.1 · Measurements

The goal was to equal or beat 3DB. The engineers ran two Lock-Houses on the same five servers, one on each foundation, and measured whole-system throughput with clients on workstations — Lock-House client, network, server and record-vault together. The tests were write-heavy, since reads are served by the master under its lease and never touch Paxos. Each worker repeatedly created a file and waited for completion.

Chapter IX

The Commission's Verdict

Despite a large literature, rites more than fifteen years old, and an experienced team — one of whom had built a similar system before — the building was significantly harder to raise than expected. The commission blamed three shortcomings of the scholars' field:

Three Shortcomings
  1. There are significant gaps between the description of Paxos and the needs of a real building. An expert must gather ideas scattered through the literature and make several small extensions of their own — and the final building rests on an unproven protocol.
  2. The field has not developed the tools that make its rites easy to build.
  3. The field has not paid enough attention to testing, a key ingredient of fault-tolerant building.

They contrasted the builders of translating-machines for languages. That field's ideas are just as difficult, yet industrial parsing tools appeared soon after the theory was understood, followed by whole families of tools for every stage; parsing, once at the cutting edge, is now taught to undergraduates as "solved". The fault-tolerance community, they concluded, had not closed its gap between theory and practice with the same vigour, and the gap merited the attention of researchers.

The later copyists summarized the verdict more bluntly than the commission ever did: the Greek parable is the first few paces of the journey; the rest is quarry management, stone sanitation, and mortar.

"Paxos Made Live" is one of the most cited descriptions of the gap between a consensus algorithm and a production system. Its catalogue — disk corruption breaking the persistence assumption, leases for local reads, leader stability, snapshots and log truncation, membership changes, and above all seeded fault-injection testing — became a checklist for later implementers. Its complaint that Paxos was hard to understand and to turn into a complete system was one of the direct motivations for Raft (Volume IX), whose authors set out to make exactly these missing pieces part of the algorithm's specification.
